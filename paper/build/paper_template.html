Honest Evaluation of Machine Learning for 2D-Material Stiffness: Structure-Family Validation, Cross-Database Testing and Universal-Potential Calibration
[Author Name(s)]
[Department, Institution, City, Country]  |  [e-mail address]
Abstract—Machine-learning surrogates for density-functional-theory (DFT) elastic constants could screen thousands of two-dimensional (2D) materials, but most reported accuracies come from random train/test splits in which near-identical polymorphs and prototype relatives sit on both sides of the split. We ask how much of that accuracy survives when whole groups are held out. Using 7,258 mechanically stable C2DB monolayers, 141 composition and geometry descriptors (no DFT outputs) and a LightGBM regressor, we compare random, chemical-system and structure-family cross-validation. The mean absolute error (MAE) of the 2D Young's modulus is 13.8 ± 0.1 N/m under random splitting and 13.9 ± 0.1 N/m when chemical systems are held out, but 19.9 ± 0.2 N/m (1.44×) when structure families are held out; adding prototype descriptors does not close this gap. Group-level ablations show that composition and geometry are complementary. Trained on all of C2DB, the model reaches MAE 19.0 N/m and Spearman 0.82 on 186 JARVIS-DFT materials, but 29.1 N/m (Spearman 0.73) on chemistries absent from C2DB; the two databases themselves differ by 8.6 N/m on shared materials. Finally, we calibrate three universal interatomic potentials against monolayer DFT stiffness with a pre-set pass/fail gate: only MACE-MPA-0 passes, and only on C2DB. Bilayer labels and models are planned but have not been produced.
Index Terms—2D materials, Young's modulus, stiffness tensor, data leakage, grouped cross-validation, gradient boosting, universal interatomic potentials

1  Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) crystals are attractive for flexible electronics, membranes and composites, where in-plane stiffness decides whether a sheet survives handling and strain. Thousands of hypothetical 2D crystals can now be generated, but measuring the stiffness of each is impossible, and computing it from first principles is expensive: every material needs a relaxed reference cell plus several strained DFT calculations. Machine-learning (ML) surrogates trained on existing DFT results promise a much cheaper estimate and have been proposed for 2D materials [[venturi,fronzi]].

How reliable are such surrogates on new materials? The common practice is to split a database at random into training and test rows. 2D databases contain many polymorphs and prototype relatives (for example MoS2, MoSe2 and WS2 share one structure type), so a random split puts close relatives of every test material into the training set. The reported error then partly measures recognition of a familiar structure type instead of prediction for an unfamiliar one. Evaluation strategies that hold out whole clusters or groups are well established in ecology and materials informatics [[roberts,meredig]], but we found none of the seven papers we reviewed that reports them for mechanical targets of 2D materials (Section 2.3).

This paper therefore treats evaluation as the object of study. We build a deliberately plain pipeline—DFT-free descriptors and three standard regressors—and stress it with progressively stricter tests. Our contributions are:

  • A cleaned set of 7,258 mechanically stable C2DB monolayers whose stability is derived from the stiffness tensor itself, not from database flags, with an audit of every filtering step (Section 4.1).
  • A leakage-aware comparison of random, cluster, chemical-system and structure-family cross-validation, with measured train/test overlap, five seeds and null baselines. Holding out structure families raises the MAE by 1.44×; holding out chemical systems does not raise it at all (Section 4.3).
  • Group-level feature ablations showing that composition and geometry are complementary and that prototype descriptors do not remove the gap (Section 4.4).
  • An external test on JARVIS-DFT with bootstrap intervals and a measured database-to-database disagreement floor (Section 4.5).
  • A pre-set-gate calibration of three universal interatomic potentials on monolayer stiffness, motivating (but not yet delivering) bilayer label generation (Section 4.6).

We state plainly at the outset what is not done: no bilayer stiffness has been computed or learned, no graph neural network or hyper-parameter search was run, and feature-level attribution (SHAP, permutation importance) was not performed.

2  Background and Related Work

2.1  Mechanical descriptors of a 2D sheet

For small in-plane strain a sheet obeys a linear stress–strain law written in Voigt notation with a 3×3 stiffness matrix over (xx, yy, xy). Because a sheet has no well-defined thickness, the stress is integrated over the cell height and the constants are quoted in N/m (force per unit edge length), not GPa. With Lz the full cell height including vacuum,

$$\sigma^{2D}_{i}=\sigma^{3D}_{i}\,L_z,\qquad C_{ij}=\frac{\partial\sigma^{2D}_i}{\partial\varepsilon_j}$$
(1)

and the in-plane Young's modulus and Poisson ratio follow from the xx and yy block:

$$Y_{2D}=\frac{C_{11}C_{22}-C_{12}^{2}}{C_{22}},\qquad \nu=\frac{C_{12}}{C_{22}}.$$
(2)

N/m scales with the number of layers whereas GPa does not, so any monolayer–bilayer comparison must state whether a value is per layer or for the whole slab; in this paper every row is a single layer (nlayers = 1), so the two coincide.

2.2  Traditional routes to stiffness

Experiment. Stiffness of suspended sheets is measured by nanoindentation or from the nonlinear response of drum resonators, for which the Duffing equation relates the resonance curves to Young's modulus [[davidovikj]]. Experimental values for the best-studied sheets carry large spreads: a review table lists graphene at 1000 ± 100 GPa and monolayer MoS2 at 270 ± 100 GPa [[zhang]]. Samples must be exfoliated or grown first, which is impractical for hypothetical materials.

Classical molecular dynamics (MD). Atomistic nanoindentation or tensile simulations with empirical potentials are fast, and have been used for graphene/MoS2 heterostructures [[elder]] and for monolayer transition-metal dichalcogenides [[malakar]]. The potential must be parameterised for each chemistry, so results transfer poorly to new compositions.

DFT finite-strain method. The reference route, used to build C2DB, relaxes the sheet, applies small strains (±1 % in xx, yy and xy), relaxes the ions at each strained cell and reads the stress. With strain amplitude δ and the unit conversion κ = 16.0218 N m−1 per eV Å−2, a central difference gives

$$C_{ij}=\kappa\,L_z\,\frac{\sigma_i(+\delta)-\sigma_i(-\delta)}{2\delta},\ \ \delta=0.01 .$$
(3)

Each material therefore needs one reference relaxation and six strained relaxations. Accurate, general and slow: this cost is what a surrogate tries to avoid.

3  Proposed Method

3.1  Data and mechanical-stability filtering

The training source is C2DB [[haastrup,gjerding]] (GPAW [[mortensen]], PBE [[pbe]]); the external source is the 2D subset of JARVIS-DFT [[choudhary20,choudhary18]] (VASP [[kresse]], OptB88vdW [[klimes]]). From each stiffness file we form the symmetrised 3×3 (xx, yy, xy) matrix C and keep a material only if it is finite, positive definite and nearly symmetric:

$$\begin{gathered}\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\tfrac12(\mathbf C+\mathbf C^{\!\top})\right)>0,\\[2pt] \frac{\max_{ij}|C_{ij}-C_{ji}|}{\max_{ij}|C_{ij}|}\le 0.10 .\end{gathered}$$
(4)

Positive definiteness is required because a sheet that does not resist stretching in every direction is mechanically unstable; excess asymmetry flags noisy or poorly converged calculations. Stability is derived from the tensor, never from the database's own label. For JARVIS only the xx, yy block is used (C11, C22 > 0 and positive determinant), because its shear entries did not reproduce known MoS2 values; corrupt tensors (NaN or denormal numbers) are dropped, not repaired. For the Poisson task rows with |ν| > 1 are removed.

3.2  Groups used for splitting

Two grouping keys are defined for every row. The chemical system is the sorted set of elements (MoS2 → Mo–S). The structure family joins the anonymised formula (which hides the element identities, so MoS2 and WSe2 both become AB2) with the layer-group number:

$$\begin{gathered}k_{\mathrm{chem}}=\mathrm{sorted}\big(\{\text{elements}\}\big),\\ k_{\mathrm{fam}}=\mathrm{anon}(\text{formula})\,\Vert\,\text{lg}\,N_{\mathrm{lg}} .\end{gathered}$$
(5)

The clean set contains 3,806 chemical systems (2,572 of them with a single member) and 676 families; the largest family has 432 rows. Polymorphs are kept as separate rows and not deduplicated, which is precisely why family-level grouping matters.

3.3  Descriptors

Each material is described by 141 numbers: 132 composition descriptors from the Magpie preset [[ward16]] computed with matminer [[ward18]] and pymatgen [[ong]], and 9 structural descriptors (lattice lengths a, b, angle γ, area per atom, atom count, slab thickness zextent, nlayers, space-group number, and number of distinct elements). In the matminer implementation, Magpie summarises 22 elemental properties with six composition-weighted statistics each (22×6 = 132); for an element property p and atomic fractions xi the mean and mean absolute deviation are

$$\bar p=\sum_i x_i\,p_i,\qquad \langle|\Delta p|\rangle=\sum_i x_i\,|p_i-\bar p| ,$$
(6)

alongside the minimum, maximum, range and mode. Because Magpie depends only on the reduced formula, two polymorphs share identical composition descriptors; the geometric descriptors are what separate them. In the present data nlayers is constant (=1) and carries no information. DFT outputs (energy above hull, formation energy, band gap, stability labels) are deliberately excluded: they require a calculation to obtain, so a model using them could not score a genuinely new material and would obscure what composition and geometry alone can explain.

3.4  Models and target transform

Stiffness is strongly right-skewed (median 47.9, mean 66.6, maximum 713.6 N/m), so the model is fitted to z = ln Y2D and predictions are mapped back with exp(·); this makes a given relative error cost the same for soft and stiff sheets. The Poisson ratio can be zero or negative and is not transformed. Three regressors from scikit-learn [[pedregosa]] and LightGBM [[ke]] are used with fixed, untuned settings: ridge regression [[hoerl]] with the penalty chosen by internal cross-validation,

$$\hat{\mathbf w}=\arg\min_{\mathbf w}\ \|\mathbf z-\mathbf X\mathbf w\|_2^2+\alpha\|\mathbf w\|_2^2 ;$$
(7)

a random forest [[breiman]] (300 trees, one third of the features per split); and LightGBM gradient-boosted trees [[ke]] (500 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, 80 % row and 50 % column subsampling), an additive model

$$F_M(\mathbf x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\eta\,f_m(\mathbf x),\quad M=500,\ \eta=0.05 .$$
(8)

All pipelines impute missing values with the median. No hyper-parameter tuning and no neural network was used.

3.5  Evaluation protocol

Five-fold cross-validation is run under four schemes: random (plain K-fold; comparison only), cluster (K-means with 20 clusters on standardised Magpie features, stratified so every cluster appears in train and test, following the idea of Fronzi et al. [[fronzi]]), chemical-system and structure-family. The two grouped schemes use stratified group K-fold on ten quantile bins of the target, which balances the target distribution across folds while keeping every group in a single fold. To make overlap explicit we measure, for each key k, the fraction of test rows whose key also occurs in training:

$$L_k=\frac{1}{|\mathcal T|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal T}\mathbf 1\!\left[k_i\in\{k_j\}_{j\in\mathcal R}\right],$$
(9)

where $\mathcal T$ is the test fold and $\mathcal R$ the training rows. Performance is reported as the mean absolute error, the coefficient of determination (also in log space, which is stable under extrapolation), and the ratio of a grouped error to the random-split error:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathrm{MAE}=\frac1n\sum_i|y_i-\hat y_i|,\qquad R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2},\\[2pt] \mathcal R_s=\frac{\mathrm{MAE}_{s}}{\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{random}}} .\end{gathered}$$
(10)

Everything is repeated for five seeds (42–46). Null baselines (global mean, chemical-system mean, family mean) show how much of a score is attributable to group identity alone. Feature ablations train the same LightGBM on subsets: all, composition only, structure only, space group only, geometry without space group, all without space group, and with layer-group or anonymous-formula one-hot columns added (categories with fewer than ten rows pooled).

3.6  External validation on JARVIS

The model is trained on all clean C2DB rows and applied to the 186 clean JARVIS rows without any JARVIS label entering training. JARVIS rows are matched to C2DB materials by formula, atom count and lattice constants within 3 % (a heuristic); this yields 106 matched rows (the material, or its twin, is in training) and 55 rows whose chemical system is absent from C2DB (unseen chemistry). Uncertainty is expressed with 95 % bootstrap intervals [[efron]] over JARVIS rows, and ranking quality with the Spearman coefficient [[spearman]] (formula valid in the absence of ties):

$$\begin{gathered}\rho=1-\frac{6\sum_i d_i^{2}}{n(n^{2}-1)},\\[2pt] d_i=\mathrm{rank}(y_i)-\mathrm{rank}(\hat y_i) .\end{gathered}$$
(11)

The agreement of the two databases on the 106 shared materials is measured the same way and serves as a noise floor for any cross-database comparison.

3.7  Calibration of universal interatomic potentials

MACE-MP-0 (“medium”), MACE-MPA-0 (“medium-mpa-0”) [[batatia]] and CHGNet 0.3.0 [[deng]] are driven through ASE [[larsen]] to reproduce the DFT protocol of Eq. (3): in-plane cell and ions are relaxed (Fmax = 0.005 eV/Å, out-of-plane length fixed), then ±1 % strains in xx, yy and xy are applied with ion relaxation. Each run first verifies that the analytic stress equals a finite-difference derivative of the energy (ratios 1.000–1.003 for all three models). The benchmark contains 150 C2DB monolayers (one random member from each of 150 randomly drawn families of the pool of 3,380 non-magnetic, Ehull ≤ 0.1 eV/atom, ≤12-atom monolayers in 395 families) and the 184 JARVIS rows with ≤24 atoms. A gate was fixed before looking at results: a potential passes only if

$$\begin{gathered}\rho\ge 0.90\ \ \wedge\ \ \mathrm{MAE}\le 1.5\,\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{DB}}=12.93\ \mathrm{N/m},\\[2pt] \mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{DB}}=8.62\ \mathrm{N/m}\ \text{(C2DB vs JARVIS labels)}.\end{gathered}$$
(12)

The thresholds are proposed standards, not physical constants. Dispersion (D3 [[grimme]]) can be switched on for the MACE interface in our code (it is not wired for CHGNet) but was not used in these stiffness runs.

4.3  Random versus grouped validation

Overlap. Fig. 3 shows why the schemes differ. Under random splitting 96 % of test rows have their structure family in training and 61 % their chemical system. The chemical-system split drives chemical overlap to zero but leaves 96 % family overlap; only the family split removes it (and keeps 59 % chemical overlap).

Accuracy. Fig. 4 and Table IV give five-seed results. LightGBM attains MAE 13.8 ± 0.1 (random), 13.9 ± 0.1 (chemical system) and 19.9 ± 0.2 N/m (family); R2 of ln Y2D falls from 0.80 to 0.68. The family/random ratio is 1.44 ± 0.02 (minimum 1.42 over seeds), 1.43 for the random forest, while the chemical-system ratio is 1.00–1.01 for both. The cluster scheme gives 1.02 (seed 42), as expected of an in-domain split.

What this shows. The random-split score is optimistic for unfamiliar structure types by roughly 44 % in error, while the model remains well above the no-feature baselines (19.9 vs 42.4 N/m). The chemical-system split, which one might expect to be the harder test, is not: removing the 61 % chemical overlap changes nothing because 96 % structural overlap remains. The inflation in this dataset therefore comes from structural similarity, not chemistry.

Is the gap just missing features? Three observations argue against it. (i) Giving the model the layer group and anonymous formula directly (Section 4.4) leaves the ratio at 1.44–1.47. (ii) The family-mean baseline falls from R2(ln) = 0.46 to 0.00 once the family is unseen, i.e. identity alone explains the random-split gain but nothing beyond it. (iii) Composition-only LightGBM still reaches R2(ln) = 0.50 on unseen families, so transferable chemical signal exists; the remaining gap is the cost of extrapolating to new prototypes.

4.4  Feature ablation

Fig. 5 removes groups of descriptors. Composition only (19.2/25.5 N/m, random/family) and structure only (19.8/26.6) perform almost identically, and combining them lowers the error by about 28 % (13.8/19.9), so the two views are complementary though partly redundant. The space-group number alone is nearly uninformative (38.0/44.3 N/m; worse than the 42.4 N/m mean predictor under family CV), and removing it from the full model costs only 0.3 N/m (14.1/20.2): the structural signal is carried by geometry. One-hot layer-group and anonymous-formula columns alone recover the family-mean baseline under random CV (R2(ln) 0.455 vs 0.463) and nothing under family CV (0.06); added to the full set they change the MAE by −0.2 to +0.1 N/m, within one to three seed standard deviations, while the family/random ratio stays at 1.44–1.47.

Limits. Ablation removes groups of columns, not single descriptors, and uses a single model; improvements below ≈0.3 N/m are not distinguishable from noise. SHAP and permutation importance were not run, so we make no claim that any individual descriptor physically determines stiffness.

4.5  Cross-database test on JARVIS

Fig. 6 and Table V report the external test. On all 186 JARVIS rows LightGBM reaches MAE 19.0 [14.4, 24.5] N/m (95 % bootstrap interval) with Spearman 0.82. On the 106 matched rows the error is 10.2 [7.9, 12.5] with Spearman 0.93—an optimistic figure, since the material is in training. On the 55 rows with chemistry absent from C2DB the error is 29.1 [20.5, 39.6] with Spearman 0.73 and R2(ln) 0.45 [−0.14, 0.75]; the lower end of this interval shows that the sample cannot rule out performance no better than the mean. The “all” row mixes leaky and non-leaky rows and is not a generalisation figure. Composition-only features collapse on unseen chemistry (R2(ln) 0.06) while structure-only features hold (0.41), but the intervals overlap, so this is suggestive only.

Interpretation. C2DB and JARVIS differ in code, functional and workflow, so even a perfect copy of C2DB would miss JARVIS by about 8.6 N/m. On matched rows the model is close to that floor (10.2). On unseen chemistry its error is roughly 3.4 times the floor, so most of that error is genuine model error, although the floor was measured on matched (possibly easier) materials. The JARVIS unit is inferred (median ratio 1.04 to C2DB), so exact agreement can never be claimed. The model is therefore useful for ranking candidates but not for quoting absolute stiffness on new chemistry. Seed changes move the MAE by only 0.4–1.1 N/m; sampling uncertainty from the small set dominates.

4.6  Universal-potential calibration (monolayers)

Table VI and Fig. 7 apply the gate of Eq. (12). MACE-MP-0 and CHGNet fail everywhere; both underestimate stiffness systematically (pooled slope through the origin 0.62 and 0.48; median predicted/DFT ratio 0.72 and 0.53). MACE-MPA-0 is the only passing combination: on the 150 C2DB monolayers it reaches Spearman 0.94 and MAE 9.9 N/m (Fig. 8). On JARVIS, however, it has Spearman 0.81 but MAE 77.5 N/m, and its pooled MAE is 47.1; the cause was not diagnosed. No potential therefore qualifies as a validated label generator at this stage.

5  Discussion

Our central finding is methodological: in this dataset, accuracy depends far more on what the test set shares with the training set than on the choice of regressor. The two tree ensembles differ by only about 1 N/m, whereas changing the split changes the MAE of the same model by about 6 N/m. Reporting a random-split number alone would have hidden this. At the same time, the model keeps real skill on unfamiliar structure families (MAE 19.9 vs 42.4 for the mean predictor) and ranks JARVIS materials reasonably (Spearman 0.73–0.82), which is what a screening tool needs.

The experiments also show where caution is needed. First, absolute values on unseen chemistry from another database can be off by roughly 30 N/m, against typical stiffnesses of 50–70 N/m. Second, the best-looking potential result (MACE-MPA-0 on C2DB) does not carry over to JARVIS, a reminder that a gate passed on one database is not a general licence to generate labels. Third, because the potentials were pretrained on large DFT collections of bulk crystals and some C2DB monolayers derive from layered bulk compounds, overlap between their training data and our benchmark has not been assessed.

5.1  Limitations

  • One training database, one DFT code and functional (C2DB: GPAW/PBE); the external set has only 186 rows, 55 of them unseen chemistry, with wide intervals.
  • JARVIS units are inferred and the C2DB–JARVIS matching is a heuristic (formula, atom count, lattice within 3 %).
  • “Family” is our own definition (anonymous formula plus layer group); other definitions could give different gaps. Family sizes are uneven (up to 432 rows), so folds are lumpy.
  • Seed standard deviations understate uncertainty from the fixed sample; polymorphs were not deduplicated.
  • Models use fixed hyper-parameters; no graph neural network was tested; ablations are group-level and single-model; no SHAP or permutation analysis was run.
  • The Poisson ratio is poorly predicted (R2 0.39 random, 0.14 family).
  • All data are monolayers. Nothing in this paper concerns bilayers or multilayers, out-of-plane constants, or bilayer binding energy; the BiDB files were not available and the corresponding code path is untested on real data.

5.2  Future work

The next stage is a bilayer extension that is planned but not yet performed: enumerate stackings of homobilayers (starting from the pool of 3,380 screened monolayers), compute in-plane stiffness and interlayer binding energy with the best-validated potential (with a dispersion correction, to be tested), quantify uncertainty by comparing two potentials, and—if DFT access allows—spot-check a few bilayers. A decisive risk, recorded in our plan, is that the stacking-induced change in in-plane stiffness may be comparable to the potential error, in which case such labels could not support a stacking-dependence claim. If labels pass these gates, the same grouped-validation protocol (grouping by monolayer and chemical system) would be applied to a bilayer model. Heterobilayers, a graph-network comparison and hyper-parameter tuning remain open.

6  Conclusion

With 141 DFT-free descriptors, LightGBM predicts the 2D Young's modulus of C2DB monolayers with an MAE of 13.8 N/m under random splitting, but 19.9 N/m (1.44×) when structure families are held out, whereas holding out chemical systems costs nothing. On an independent database the model ranks materials well but is only roughly quantitative on new chemistry, and no universal potential yet passes our gate on both databases. Honest, group-aware evaluation should therefore precede any extension to bilayers.
